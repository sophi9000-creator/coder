    <h1>Finding My Voice: Four Lessons That Changed How I Understand Writing</h1>
    <p><strong>Genre:</strong> Blog Post | <strong>Audience:</strong> First-year college students unsure about writing | <strong>Purpose:</strong> To build confidence, flexibility, and clarity in writing</p>
</header>

<div class="container">

    <h2>Introduction</h2>
    <p>
        When I started college, I honestly thought writing was something other people were naturally good at — and I definitely wasn’t one of them. Writing was never an easy process for me. I used to reread every sentence over and over before even finishing a paragraph, constantly changing things because I felt insecure about my writing.
    </p>
    <p>
        But throughout this class, my mindset started shifting. I realized writing isn’t about perfection, and it definitely isn’t about trying to sound like someone you’re not. Writing is something you grow into — a tool you reshape each time you return to a draft, each time you listen to feedback, and each time you allow your own voice to show up on the page.
    </p>
    <p>
        This blog is for new students who feel the same anxiety and pressure I used to feel. I want to share the four lessons that changed not only how I write, but how I understand myself as a communicator.
    </p>

    <!-- CLAIM 1 -->
    <div class="claim">
        <h2>Claim 1: Writing is a process—not a one-time performance.</h2>
        <p>
            If I’m being honest, I used to stare at a blank screen for way too long, convinced the first sentence had to be perfect or the whole paper would fall apart. That pressure made writing feel impossible. My biggest breakthrough came when I learned that good writing starts messy.
        </p>
        <p>
            As Anne Lamott explains in “Shitty First Drafts,” almost every writer — even famous ones — begin with something rough. That idea took so much weight off my shoulders. Once I let myself draft freely, writing stopped feeling like a test and started feeling like discovery.
        </p>
        <p>
            I realized I could explore ideas, come back later, and shape them into something better. Writing isn’t always pretty, and sometimes those messy drafts look useless — but they’re the foundation of everything. Revision became the most impactful part of my writing journey. The moment I stopped treating writing like a performance, I finally started learning how to write.
        </p>
    </div>

    <!-- CLAIM 2 -->
    <div class="claim">
        <h2>Claim 2: My language background isn’t a weakness—it’s part of my strength.</h2>
        <p>
            Growing up, I moved between different ways of speaking at home, with friends, and in school. But in academic settings, I felt pressure to hide those voices. I thought “good writing” had to sound polished and formal, even if it didn’t sound like me at all.
        </p>
        <p>
            Reading Amy Tan’s “Mother Tongue” completely changed the way I saw my language background. Tan explains how the English she grew up hearing — what others called “broken” — wasn’t broken at all. It carried emotion, culture, and truth. It shaped her worldview and her writing.
        </p>
        <p>
            That really stuck with me. It helped me understand that the different Englishes I use aren’t a problem — they’re part of my identity. My language background gives me flexibility, perspective, and an authentic voice on the page. I don’t need to erase where I come from to fit academic expectations. My voice is already enough.
        </p>
    </div>

    <!-- CLAIM 3 -->
    <div class="claim">
        <h2>Claim 3: Feedback is not a judgment of my intelligence—it’s a tool for growth.</h2>
        <p>
            I used to take every comment on my writing personally, as if every suggestion meant I wasn’t smart enough. Peer review felt intimidating at first, especially because it happened constantly in our English class — sharing drafts, swapping papers, giving honest feedback.
        </p>
        <p>
            But seeing other people struggle made the process less isolating. Little by little, feedback stopped feeling like criticism and started feeling like guidance. Comments helped me understand how real readers interpreted my ideas.
        </p>
        <p>
            Peer review is what helped me grow the most. Every time I revised — clarifying a point, tightening a paragraph, or reworking a confusing sentence — my writing became more intentional. I learned that strong writers don’t fear feedback; they use it.
        </p>
    </div>

    <!-- CLAIM 4 -->
    <div class="claim">
        <h2>Claim 4: Purpose and audience are the real foundation of strong writing.</h2>
        <p>
            One of the most helpful things I’ve learned is that writing becomes much easier once you understand who you’re speaking to and why. When your purpose and audience are clear, your writing gains direction.
        </p>
        <p>
            In class, we learned about the “rhetorical situation,” which means every text exists for a reason and for specific readers. When I applied that idea, writing stopped feeling like filling space and started feeling intentional.
        </p>
        <p>
            We practiced changing our audience — from classmates, to instructors, to community groups — and each shift changed the tone, vocabulary, and evidence we used. Amy Tan does the same thing in “Mother Tongue,” switching voices depending on who she’s addressing.
        </p>
        <p>
            Writing to incoming students feels completely different than writing to a professor — and that’s a strength, not a weakness. Understanding purpose and audience is what finally made writing enjoyable.
        </p>
    </div>

    <!-- CONCLUSION -->
    <h2>Conclusion</h2>
    <p>
        If there’s one thing I want incoming college students to know, it’s that writing isn’t something you’re born good at — it’s something you grow into. These four lessons changed how I see myself as a writer, and they can help you too.
    </p>
    <p>
        First, don’t fear the messy beginning — strong writing starts with imperfect drafts. Second, your language background is part of your strength, and it shapes your unique voice. Third, feedback isn’t a judgment; it’s one of the most powerful tools for growth. And finally, everything becomes easier when you understand your purpose and audience.
    </p>
    <p>
        Writing doesn’t have to be perfect to be powerful. It just has to be yours.
    </p>
